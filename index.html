Write-up

Climate projections are often reported through a single global number, such as an average temperature increase by 2100. However, these averages don't show how warming can differ across individual regions and states. For our project, we wanted to explore how projected temperature increases vary across the 48 contiguous U.S. states and how those projections change depending on emissions scenarios and climate models. We initially wanted to compare individual states' climate projections based on political differences, but we realized that geography and regional climate patterns would make those comparisons difficult to interpret in the context of our dataset. Thus, we decided to focus on building an interactive map that allows users to compare regions, explore different emissions scenarios, and examine the uncertainty across climate models at the state level. Through this visualization, users can identify which states are projected to experience the greatest warming and see how those projections change across scenarios and future time periods.

For our visualization, we used climate simulations from the CMIP6 dataset. We selected five climate models and focused on monthly near-surface air temperatures under two emissions scenarios: SSP2-4.5, representing intermediate emissions, and SSP5-8.5, representing very high emissions. Using Python, we converted the monthly temperatures into annual averages, interpolated the model data onto a common geographic grid, and calculated area-weighted temperature averages for each of the 48 contiguous U.S. states. We then calculated projected warming relative to each model's own historical simulation from 1981–2010. For each model, we averaged the warming over three 20-year periods: 2021–2040, 2041–2060, and 2081–2100. Finally, we took the median across the five models to obtain the values displayed on our map.

We chose a choropleth map because our goal was to compare projected warming across individual U.S. states. Although an isopleth map could better represent continuous temperature patterns, we wanted users to compare familiar geographic regions. We used D3's Albers USA equal-area projection to preserve relative state areas while displaying the map. For temperature changes, we used a sequential color scale, with darker colors representing greater warming. After receiving feedback about just noticeable differences (JND) in our checkpoint, we replaced our original orange palette with reversed Inferno to improve color distinguishability. We also kept the scale fixed from 1–7°C across every scenario and time period, allowing users to make direct comparisons. Although this reduces the visibility of smaller differences in near-term projections, it ensures that colors retain consistent meanings. Finally, we included numerical labels and callouts for smaller Northeastern states to reduce the difficulty of reading small regions.

We added several interactive features to help users explore the climate projections beyond what a static map could show. Users can switch between two emissions scenarios and three future time periods, allowing them to observe how projected warming changes over time and how the scenarios begin to diverge toward the end of the century. We also included a dynamic subtitle that updates with each selection, highlighting important patterns in the data and helping users understand what to look for in the map. We chose buttons instead of sliders or dropdown menus because there were only three time periods and two emissions scenarios, making all the available options easy to see and select. Hovering over a state reveals its median warming, its ranking among the 48 states, and the difference between the two emissions scenarios. The hovered state is also outlined on the map and highlighted on the color legend, making it easier to compare against other states. The tooltip also displays the range of projections across our five climate models. We included this range because the median alone doesn't show how much the models disagree about the magnitude of warming. Together, these interactions allow users to investigate individual states more precisely while still identifying broader geographic patterns.

There were various limitations and trade-offs we considered while developing our visualization. Rather than showing temperature projections for every individual year, we grouped the data into three 20-year periods to reduce the influence of year-to-year variability and make long-term warming patterns easier to compare. One limitation of our choropleth is that each state is represented by a single average, which can hide important differences within larger states. Additionally, we chose to focus on the 48 contiguous U.S. states, meaning our findings do not represent projected warming in Alaska or Hawaii. Finally, our analysis uses only five climate models with one simulation each, so the range of projections reflects differences among those models rather than the full uncertainty associated with future climate change.

Shaun initially explored climate projections based on political affiliations and helped develop the project's direction. Srinivas worked on processing the climate data at the state level, producing the exploratory visualizations for our checkpoint, and implementing the interactive D3 website. As we moved toward the final submission, Shaun and Abhinav worked on polishing and simplifying the visualization, reducing unnecessary text and emphasizing visuals to make it more straightforward for the audience. Corey provided feedback on the visualization and helped revise the final write-up. We also worked together to review the project's design choices and identify improvements. Overall, our group spent approximately 10–15 person-hours across the checkpoint and final submission, including data processing, visualization development, testing, design refinement, and writing.
